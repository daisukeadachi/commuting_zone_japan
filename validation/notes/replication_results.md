# Fowler (2024) replicated on the Japanese delineation

What follows reports every table and figure of <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-024-03829-5> that the available data supports, computed on Japanese census commuting flows for 1980 to 2020. The pairwise wage correlation is absent, since the wage microdata application is pending. Tables are under `validation/output` and figures under `validation/output/figures`; the code that builds them is under `validation/code` and runs in the order `build_scope_and_adjacency.R`, `build_dissimilarity.R`, `build_clusters.R`, `make_diagnostics.R`, `make_containment.R`, `make_similarity.R`, `make_core.R`, `make_reassignments.R`, `make_figures.R`.

## What the delineation is built from

The commuting matrix is the "mainly working" variant restricted to ordinary households, which is the only definition available for 2020. The dissimilarity between two municipalities is one minus the sum of the two directed commuting flows over the smaller of the two resident labour forces, capped so that no dissimilarity falls below 0.001. The tree is built by average linkage and cut at a fixed height. Two cutoffs are carried throughout, 0.977 from the source paper and 0.980 from the existing Japanese pipeline. The baseline is 0.977, and every number and figure below is at that value unless stated; the tables carry both.

The sample is 1,656 municipalities, those on the four main islands or the Okinawa main island together with those joined to one of them by a permanent road link. The twenty-three special wards of Tokyo count as a single municipality, as the wards of the ordinance-designated cities do. Area and compactness are computed on a Japan-wide equal-area conic projection. Population throughout is the resident labour force carried in the commuting matrix, because actual census population by municipality exists locally for one year only; every table that reports population states this.

Treating the wards as one unit matters in two places. It is the unit the Urban Employment Area delineation itself uses, under the code 13100, so the Core measures now match their source; with the wards separate none of them matched any Urban Employment Area member, and the largest core in the country was absent from the Core block. And it removes an artifact of the proportional flow measure, which divides by the smaller of two resident labour forces and so reads a suburb's commute into twenty-three separate destinations as twenty-three weak links rather than one strong one.

One departure from the existing Japanese pipeline is deliberate and material. That pipeline builds its bilateral flow table with a join that keeps a pair only when the flow from the first municipality to the second is recorded, so a pair connected only in the reverse direction enters as unconnected. The matrix it clusters is therefore asymmetric. The replication uses the symmetric numerator that the source method specifies. `validation/notes/method_crosscheck.md` measures what that changes, on the pipeline's own municipality universe.

## Diagnostic statistics

`validation/output/diagnostics_table.csv` carries the full table for every census year at both cutoffs. The delineation consolidates steadily, from 384 commuting zones in 1980 to 223 in 2020, while the count of single-municipality zones falls from 71 to 10 and mean zone area grows from 947 to 1,630 square kilometres. The most populous zone in 2020 holds 5.62 million workers across 26 municipalities: the special wards together with Kawasaki, Hachioji, Machida and the inner Tama cities, Kawaguchi, Toda and Warabi to the north, and Ichikawa and Urayasu across the river. The zone holding the most municipalities is a different one, the twenty-nine around Nagoya, which carries 1.85 million workers.

Compactness, the Polsby-Popper ratio of the dissolved zone averaged across zones, sits between 0.30 and 0.33 in every year and drifts slowly downward as zones grow. The choice of projection barely touches it: across the 47 prefecture shapes the ratio moves by at most 0.2 percent between an equal-area conic, an equal-area azimuthal, a Transverse Mercator zone and geodesic measurement on the ellipsoid.

## Containment

`validation/output/containment_table.csv` carries the Containment block. Mean containment across zones, the share of a municipality's residents who work inside its own commuting zone averaged over municipalities and then over zones, sits at 0.904 in 2020 and has moved little since 1980, when it was 0.911. Weighting by resident labour force lowers it to 0.870, and the share of the national labour force that lives and works in the same zone is 0.861. Work containment, the share of jobs filled from inside the zone, is 0.914 in 2020.

The zone with the lowest mean containment in 2020 is a six-municipality cluster in southern Saitama, Niiza, Asaka, Fujimi, Fujimino, Shiki and Miyoshi, at 0.405 over 227,000 workers, with Asaka the least contained municipality in the country at 0.338. `lowest_containment_zone_2020_cut0.977.png` shades every municipality of that zone and of the zones adjacent to it by its own containment, over a basemap, with the zone boundaries drawn heavy and numbered and the least contained municipality named. These are commuter towns whose residents work in the Tokyo zone next to them; the proportional flow keeps them together because they resemble one another, and the denominator of the smaller labour force keeps them from joining the far larger neighbour. The same mechanism produces the low-containment cases the source paper reports for the United States.

## Similarity between delineations and the cutoff

`similarity_map_2010_2020_cut0.977.png` maps the municipality-level Jaccard similarity between the 2010 and the 2020 delineations, and `validation/output/similarity_table.csv` reports the summaries. Mean similarity for that pair is 0.850. One pre-pandemic control is higher and one is level with it: 0.883 for 2010 against 2015 and 0.850 for 2005 against 2015. Decade churn was larger further back, at 0.772 for 1980 against 1990 and 0.834 for 1990 against 2000, so the 2010 to 2020 figure sits inside the historical range rather than outside it.

The cutoff sweep is the central exercise, and its result is the flatness the design anticipated. `similarity_vs_cutoff_2010_2020_anchor0.977.png` shows the curve. Holding 2010 at 0.977 and varying the 2020 cutoff, mean similarity peaks at 0.853 at a cutoff of 0.975, and stays within 0.005 of that peak over the range 0.974 to 0.977, which spans 236 down to 223 commuting zones. Anchoring 2010 at 0.980 instead moves the peak to 0.978 and the plateau to 0.977 to 0.979, spanning 223 down to 216 zones. The two anchors therefore sit inside or immediately beside each other's plateau, and nothing in the objective distinguishes them. The same pattern holds for both control pairs.

The diagnostic profile over the same range is in `zone_count_vs_cutoff_2020.png`, `containment_vs_cutoff_2020.png` and `noncontiguous_zones_vs_cutoff_2020.png`, and shows what a move in the cutoff costs. Zone count falls steeply as the cutoff rises while containment rises, so the cutoff trades granularity against self-containment with no interior optimum of its own. This supports reporting a defensible range rather than a single value.

## Contiguity

Non-contiguity is rare in Japan. The number of zones holding a municipality that shares its zone with none of its neighbours is two in 1980 and 1985, one in 1990, two in 1995, one in 2000, zero in 2005 and 2010, two in 2015 and one in 2020. The affected labour force never exceeds 0.072 percent of the national total, and in 2020 it is 0.002 percent. `noncontiguous_municipalities_2020_cut0.977.png` maps the 2020 case and `validation/output/reassignment_table.csv` lists every case with the alternative that would remove it.

The recurring cases are mountain municipalities whose road connections run away from their land neighbours. Gujō and Shirakawa in Gifu appear in every year from 1980 to 2000, Uenohara in Yamanashi in 1980, 1995 and 2015, Kawai in Nara in 1985, and Shimukappu in Hokkaido in 2015 and 2020. In every case the assigned zone receives far more of the municipality's commuters than any adjacent zone does, between 80 and 97 percent against no more than 10.4 percent, so reassignment would trade a contiguous map for a worse fit to the flows. That is the diagnostic's answer: the non-contiguity is a property of the commuting pattern, not a failure of the clustering, and the delineation keeps every municipality where the clustering put it.

The adjacency graph itself was checked for the generalization artifacts that would manufacture false non-contiguity. Among the 4,531 municipality pairs whose polygons lie within one kilometre of each other, 171 do not share a boundary, and none of those sits closer than 269 metres. The layer has no hairline gaps between municipalities that genuinely adjoin, so every detachment reported above is real geography.

## Core

`validation/output/core_table.csv` carries the Core block on two definitions. On the Urban Employment Area central cities, 67.3 percent of the 2020 commuting zones contain a core, 51.9 percent of workers commute to a core inside their own zone, and 47.7 percent of jobs are held by residents of a core inside the zone. On the mechanical rule that a municipality is a core when its densely inhabited district population reaches 10,000, which uses no commuting data at all, the same three measures read 73.1, 74.4 and 73.9 percent; at the 50,000 threshold they read 45.7, 61.3 and 60.4 percent.

The two definitions agree on direction and on time trend but not on level, and the gap is informative rather than troubling. The Urban Employment Area marks a single central city per area and excludes any municipality that is already a suburb of another, so its core set is smaller and more concentrated than a threshold rule that marks every municipality with a large enough dense population. Reading the two together is the point: the district rule cannot be re-reading the commuting flows the delineation was built from, and it moves the same way over time, so the Core measures are picking up urban structure rather than the delineation's own inputs.

`split_urban_areas_2020_cut0.977.png` maps the fourth measure, laying the split urban areas over the commuting zones that split them so that each area is seen straddling a zone boundary; areas that fall inside one zone are not drawn. Of the 208 Urban Employment Areas in 2020, 33 are split across more than one commuting zone. The count fell from 55 in 2000 to 31 in 2010 and has been roughly flat since, which tracks the consolidation of the zones themselves.

## What is not here

The sensitivity check on the sample definition, using all employed persons whose workplace is identifiable rather than those mainly working, waits on the disaggregated 2020 file.
